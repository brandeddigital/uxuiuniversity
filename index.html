Questions we've answered

How big should tap targets be?

BD-046 sets the WCAG 2.2 AA floor at 24×24 CSS px, with a spacing exception. BD-045 recommends about 1cm physical size, which is considerably larger on most phones. Our view: treat 24px as the compliance minimum, design primary actions toward the physical size, then check mis-taps on real devices.

Should the homepage hero rotate?

BD-054 reports that later carousel slides often go unseen and advises against auto-rotation on mobile. BD-050 requires a pause control whenever content moves, and BD-049 lets people turn off interaction-triggered motion. Our view: one strong static message is the safer default; if you rotate, give people the controls. Neither source measures conversion.

What makes form errors recoverable?

BD-040 covers message wording and placement, BD-041 adds a linked summary at the top of the page, and BD-025 warns against rejecting valid variations. Our view: audit wording, location and acceptance rules together. These are complementary guidance sources, not independent experiments.

Should we hide detail to simplify a page?

BD-030 supports deferring rarely used controls; BD-001 and BD-005 emphasise readily available purchase information; BD-059 shows attention still concentrates near the top. Our view: keep decision-critical information visible early and test access to the rest.

Is a fast site enough?

BD-015 defines performance measures; BD-057 and BD-058 cover how waiting feels; BD-035 sets historical response-time expectations. Our view: assess loading, feedback during waits and task completion separately. A fast page can still be confusing.

    How we check sources

    Every entry is written from the original source, in our own words. We record who published it, when, how they got their result and who they studied. Where a source doesn't say, the entry says "Not stated" rather than guessing.

    How strong is the evidence?

    Every entry carries a strength rating, so an audit can lead with its best-backed points. It answers one question: how confident can you be that this change affects what people actually do?

    • StrongA controlled experiment (an A/B test or randomised study) or a large real-world dataset, with the sample and period stated. Lead with these.
    • ModerateMeasured, but not controlled: usability testing, eyetracking, expert benchmarks, surveys, single-site case studies and natural experiments. Good evidence of a problem, weaker evidence of the fix's size.
    • Guidance onlyStandards, laws, platform documentation, design-system patterns and expert advice. A WCAG rule or a legal requirement can still be mandatory; this rating only says the source doesn't measure what the change does to behaviour.

    Kinds of evidence

    Each entry is also grouped by what kind of claim its source can support.

    • Measured researchUsability testing, eyetracking, benchmarks, audits and case studies. Supports claims about observed behaviour in a defined sample. Match the sample, period and device before applying it. Case studies are not randomised evidence.
    • Standards & specsWCAG, W3C tutorials, platform documentation, design-system patterns and regulatory reports. Reliable for what is required or specified. Not proof of business uplift.
    • Expert guidanceHeuristics, syntheses and method advice. Useful for planning and structuring reviews. Open the original before quoting any number.

    Editorial rules

    1. Open the original source before adding or citing an entry.
    2. Write every finding in our own words. Store a statistic only with its definition and the study it came from.
    3. Several articles from one research programme count as one evidence base, not as independent confirmation.
    4. Audit actions and measures are our suggestions, not tested guarantees. A case-study uplift is never a general promise.
    5. Entries marked Method not yet checked have a reviewed summary, but we haven't yet recorded the source's method and sample. Check the original before quoting them.

    Plain English and pictures

    Entries are being rewritten so the point lands fast: a one-line summary anyone can follow, a picture of the rule in action, and a Do / Don't pair. The full finding and its limits stay one tap away. Pictures are illustrations of the rule, not screenshots or measured data. They're drawn on a phone or a laptop, depending on where the source studied the behaviour. Entries marked Illustrated have been converted; the rest follow over the coming weeks.
